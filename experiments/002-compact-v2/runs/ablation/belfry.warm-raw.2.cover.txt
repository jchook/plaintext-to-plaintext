I walked down the street, past the worn brick façade of Mama Jean's Café, its awning a faded turquoise that clashed with the new, sterile blue paint on the opposite building. The aroma of freshly brewed coffee wafted from Mama Jean's windows and mingled with the smell of yesterday's fried chicken from the food cart parked outside town hall. I picked up a breakfast sandwich on a roll patterned with golden brown threads, like a threadbare rug.

Inside the barbershop, Jerry regaled a customer, Leon, with stories of his childhood in this town – small-town boy, now a proud father of two kids, both with the same stubborn habit of crossing their arms when they didn't want to talk. Behind the counter, Jerry's barber shears hung on a pegboard beside a glass case housing vintage broaches. The cashier, Tina, a teenager with skin as smooth as birchbark, printed Leon's receipt, scribbled in her black pen that moved with a swift, decisive flip.

The scent of old fabric wafted from the vintage clothing store – faded florals on the curtains, faded pastries in the display window. Emma, the owner, restocked baskets of plush pink throw pillows, their edges frayed like the edges of a well-loved book.
