I walked down the strip of Main Street, where bright yellow signs for the local diner and the antique shop stood out against the drab grey concrete. The diner, aptly named Sunny Days, was bustling with customers fueling up for a day's work on the farm just outside town. I caught the scent of old coffee wafting out into the street, drawing me in with its warm, comforting aroma.

Inside, waitress Emily Jenkins moved with a practiced ease, taking orders from the mostly elderly patrons. She had a laugh that was deepened by years of working in this diner, and it put everyone at ease. I noticed a woman sitting alone at the counter, sipping a cup of coffee under the gaze of a stained glass window depicting a scenic Vermont landscape. The woman, whose name tag read Mrs. LaPierre, seemed lost in thought.

Outside, a young girl in a yellow sundress was playing alone with her stuffed rabbit at the antique shop. The store was run by Joe Wilson, the town's eccentric proprietor. His shirts reaked of mildew, and the air inside was thick with old books and vintage trinkets. As I helped a customer find a vintage harmonica, I couldn't help but glance over at the girl, who seemed entirely absorbed in a reenactment of a World War II battle.

Next to the antique shop stood a bakery called Butter & Brioche. The sign above the door read "Today's Special: Cranberry Orange Scones." My stomach growled with anticipation, and I ducked inside to sample a few. The shop owner, Rachel Taylor, smiled warmly at me. She made her daily bread fresh, mixing in a perfect balance of sugar and tangy orange zest. For $3.50, two of these warm, flaky wonders were mine.
